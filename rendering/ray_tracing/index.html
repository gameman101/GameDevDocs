    Ray Tracing

    Ray tracing is a rendering technique that simulates how light behaves in the real world by tracing the path of light rays as they interact with surfaces. Unlike rasterization which approximates lighting, ray tracing physically models reflection, refraction, shadows, and global illumination.

    Core Ray Tracing

    Whitted Ray Tracing

    Introduced by Turner Whitted in 1980 in his SIGGRAPH paper "An Improved Illumination Model for Shaded Display", this was the first recursive ray tracing algorithm and fundamentally changed how rendering was approached.

    A primary ray is cast from the camera through each pixel into the scene. When it hits a surface, secondary rays are spawned recursively: - Shadow rays cast toward each light to check for occlusion - Reflection rays for mirror-like surfaces - Refraction rays for transparent surfaces using Snell's law and the surface's index of refraction

    At diffuse surfaces, a standard illumination model like Phong is used. The algorithm recurses until it hits a diffuse surface or a maximum depth is reached.

    Limitations: Whitted ray tracing only handles perfect specular reflection and refraction. It cannot simulate soft shadows, glossy reflections, depth of field, or motion blur since all rays travel in precise, single directions.

    Distributed Ray Tracing

    Introduced by Robert Cook in 1984, distributed ray tracing extends Whitted by distributing ray directions stochastically according to the function they are sampling rather than firing a single precise ray.

    By randomly distributing rays across: - Area lights → produces soft shadows with penumbras - Reflection lobes → produces glossy/blurry reflections - Camera aperture → produces depth of field - Time → produces motion blur - Lens → produces antialiasing naturally

    This was a major step toward physically accurate rendering. Multiple rays are traced per pixel and averaged, so image quality increases with more samples at the cost of render time.

    Path Tracing

    Path tracing is an unbiased Monte Carlo rendering algorithm that solves the full rendering equation. Rather than recursively branching rays like Whitted, a single ray path is traced from the camera and bounced through the scene randomly according to the surface's BSDF until it either hits a light or is terminated by Russian Roulette. Many paths are traced per pixel and averaged to converge to a correct solution.

    Unidirectional Path Tracing

    The standard form of path tracing. Paths are traced starting from the camera, bouncing off surfaces until they reach a light source. Simple to implement and works well in most scenes, but is inefficient in scenes with small or hard-to-reach light sources since most paths never find them.

    Weaknesses: Caustics, interior scenes with small windows, and any lighting configuration where light paths are difficult to hit by chance from the camera.

    Bidirectional Path Tracing (BDPT)

    Constructs two subpaths simultaneously — one from the camera and one from a light source — then attempts to connect them with a visibility ray in the middle. This dramatically improves efficiency in scenes where light is difficult to reach from the camera side alone.

    BDPT is unbiased, meaning it converges to the mathematically correct solution with enough samples. It handles tricky lighting like interior scenes, indirect illumination, and caustics far more efficiently than unidirectional path tracing.

    Metropolis Light Transport (MLT)

    MLT is a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method. Instead of sampling paths independently, it starts with a found light-carrying path and mutates it slightly to explore nearby paths. Paths that carry more light are sampled more frequently.

    This makes MLT extremely efficient in difficult scenes with complex lighting — for example a scene lit only through a tiny crack, or a scene full of caustics where standard path tracing would rarely find any light-carrying paths at all.

    Trade-off: MLT produces correlated noise rather than independent noise, which can look like splotchy artifacts. It is also harder to implement and parallelize than standard path tracing.

    Progressive Path Tracing

    A refinement strategy rather than a distinct algorithm. The renderer continuously adds more samples per pixel, updating the image progressively in real time. Each new pass adds more information and the image visually converges from noisy to clean over time.

    Used heavily in offline rendering applications like Blender's Cycles renderer, allowing artists to preview lighting interactively while the image refines in the background.

    Photon-Based Methods

    Photon Mapping

    A two-pass biased rendering algorithm introduced by Henrik Wann Jensen. In the first pass, photons are emitted from light sources and traced through the scene. When they hit diffuse surfaces their position, direction, and energy are stored in a spatial data structure called a photon map (typically a kd-tree).

    In the second pass, rays are traced from the camera. At each surface intersection, nearby photons in the photon map are used to estimate the local irradiance via density estimation.

    Photon mapping excels at caustics and scenes involving complex light transport that path tracing struggles with, such as light focusing through glass. It is a biased algorithm, meaning it does not converge to the exact correct solution, but it is consistent — accuracy increases with more photons.

    Limitation: The entire photon map must be held in memory, placing a hard cap on quality.

    Progressive Photon Mapping (PPM)

    Solves the memory limitation of standard photon mapping. PPM begins with a ray tracing pass from the camera, then iteratively shoots more and more photon passes, progressively refining the radiance estimate at each visible point.

    Because the full photon map does not need to be stored — only statistics at hit points are accumulated — PPM can run with any desired accuracy using a fixed, limited amount of memory. It converges to the correct solution over time, making it practical for scenes with caustics and complex light transport where path tracing is inefficient.

    Volumetric / Participating Media

    Volumetric Ray Tracing / Ray Marching

    Used to render participating media — volumes where light scatters and absorbs as it travels through the medium, such as fog, smoke, clouds, fire, and subsurface skin.

    A ray is marched in small steps through the volume. At each step the algorithm accounts for: - Absorption → light lost as it passes through the medium - Scattering → light deflected in a new direction - Emission → light emitted by the medium itself (fire, glowing gas)

    Phase functions (such as Henyey-Greenstein) describe the angular distribution of scattered light. Volumetric ray tracing is computationally expensive and is typically combined with importance sampling and noise reduction techniques.

    Specialised / Hybrid Variants

    Cone Tracing

    Instead of an infinitely thin ray, a cone is traced through the scene. The cone expands as it travels, covering a larger area of the scene the farther it goes. This approximates anti-aliasing and filtering naturally since the cone footprint represents the solid angle being sampled.

    Used in some global illumination approximations like voxel cone tracing (VXGI), where a voxelized representation of the scene is sampled with cones to estimate indirect lighting efficiently.

    Beam Tracing

    A generalization of cone tracing that uses beams of arbitrary cross-sectional shape instead of cones. Beams can represent the exact projection of a pixel through the scene, accounting for all geometry they intersect rather than just the nearest hit.

    Used in acoustic simulation and some specialized rendering contexts where full coverage of a beam's path matters.

    Irradiance Caching / Radiance Caching

    A caching strategy to avoid recomputing expensive indirect lighting at every surface point. Irradiance is computed at a sparse set of points in the scene and interpolated for nearby points that have similar lighting conditions.

    This provides a massive speedup for indirect diffuse lighting in offline rendering at the cost of some accuracy in areas with rapidly changing illumination. Radiance caching extends this idea to non-diffuse surfaces by caching directional radiance rather than just irradiance.

    Vertex Connection and Merging (VCM)

    A unified algorithm that combines Bidirectional Path Tracing and Progressive Photon Mapping into a single framework using Multiple Importance Sampling (MIS). It automatically weights the contributions of both strategies to minimize noise.

    VCM is particularly effective at specular-diffuse-specular (SDS) light paths — for example light reflecting off a mirror onto a diffuse floor and then into a glass object — which are difficult for either BDPT or photon mapping alone.

    Adjoint / Importance-Driven Path Tracing

    Rather than tracing paths from the camera, adjoint methods trace paths from the light sources toward regions of importance (such as the camera or a specific region of interest). The adjoint of the rendering equation is solved instead of the forward equation.

    This is mathematically equivalent to forward path tracing but can be more efficient in scenes where light transport from the source is more predictable than from the camera. Bidirectional methods combine both forward and adjoint tracing.

    Real-Time Variants (GPU-Accelerated)

    Real-Time Ray Tracing (DXR / Vulkan RT)

    Hardware-accelerated ray tracing introduced with NVIDIA's Turing architecture (RTX 20 series) in 2018. Exposed through Microsoft's DirectX Raytracing (DXR) API and Vulkan's VK_KHR_ray_tracing extension.

    Dedicated hardware units called RT Cores handle bounding volume hierarchy (BVH) traversal and ray-triangle intersection tests, offloading this from shader cores. This makes selective ray tracing feasible in real-time at interactive framerates.

    In games, full path tracing is still too expensive so ray tracing is typically used selectively for specific effects: - Ray-traced shadows - Ray-traced reflections - Ray-traced ambient occlusion - Ray-traced global illumination

    These are blended with rasterized geometry to produce a hybrid result. Denoising (using AI denoisers like NVIDIA DLSS or dedicated NRD denoisers) is essential since only a very small number of rays per pixel can be traced in real time.

    Game Engines and Their Ray Tracing Types

    Godot (4.x)

    Godot does not have built-in hardware ray tracing support. Its rendering is based on rasterization with screen-space techniques for reflections and ambient occlusion. External ray tracing solutions or custom render plugins could theoretically be integrated but there is no official ray tracing pipeline in Godot as of version 4.x.

    Unity

    Unity's ray tracing is exclusive to the High Definition Render Pipeline (HDRP). It requires a DXR-capable GPU (NVIDIA RTX or AMD RDNA2+) and DirectX 12.

    Ray-Traced Ambient Occlusion (RTAO)

    Replaces screen-space AO with physically accurate ambient occlusion computed by tracing rays into the hemisphere above each surface point. Produces correct occlusion in corners, crevices, and around objects without the screen-space artifacts of SSAO.

    Ray-Traced Reflections (RTR)

    Replaces screen-space reflections (SSR) with ray-traced reflections that correctly reflect geometry off-screen, at grazing angles, and behind the camera. Supports roughness for glossy reflections.

    Ray-Traced Global Illumination (RTGI)

    Computes indirect diffuse lighting by tracing rays into the scene from each surface and gathering incoming light. Produces accurate color bleeding and bounce lighting that baked lightmaps or probes cannot match for dynamic scenes.

    Ray-Traced Shadows

    Traces shadow rays toward area lights to produce physically correct soft shadows with accurate penumbras based on light size and distance. Replaces shadow maps which cannot represent soft shadows accurately.

    Path Tracer

    A full offline-quality path tracer available in HDRP for producing ground-truth reference images. Not intended for real-time gameplay but used for cinematic renders, look development, and reference comparisons.

    Unreal Engine

    Lumen (UE5's Default GI System)

    Lumen is UE5's fully dynamic global illumination and reflections system. It operates using a hybrid approach — screen traces for nearby geometry and hardware ray tracing or signed distance field (SDF) traces for distant geometry, all feeding into a surface cache that stores and reuses lighting data.

    Lumen provides real-time indirect lighting, color bleeding, and reflections that update dynamically as lights and geometry change, without baking. It scales from software-only mode (no RT hardware required) to full hardware ray tracing mode for higher quality.

    Legacy / Explicit Hardware Ray Tracing Effects (UE4 / UE5)

    Available since UE4.22, these are direct per-effect hardware ray tracing features that can be enabled individually in the post process volume: - Ray-Traced Shadows - Ray-Traced Reflections - Ray-Traced Ambient Occlusion - Ray-Traced Translucency - Ray-Traced Global Illumination (RTGI, largely superseded by Lumen in UE5)

    These give artists explicit control over which effects use ray tracing, useful when Lumen's automatic approach does not fit the project's needs.

    Path Tracer

    UE5 includes a full hardware path tracer for offline-quality rendering, used for cinematic shots, archviz, and reference rendering. It uses progressive accumulation and converges to a physically correct solution over many frames. In UE5.4+ it supports Nanite geometry for film-quality geometric detail.

    NVIDIA RTX Branch (NvRTX)

    NvRTX is a custom fork of Unreal Engine maintained by NVIDIA on GitHub, containing the latest experimental and production ray tracing technologies ahead of their inclusion in the main UE branch. It is fully compatible with the corresponding UE version.

    Key technologies included in NvRTX:

    • RTXGI (RTX Global Illumination) — Computes infinite multi-bounce indirect lighting and soft shadow occlusion dynamically without bake times or light leaks. Available in NvRTX 4.27 and 5.0.
    • RTXDI (RTX Direct Illumination) — Allows an unlimited number of dynamic shadow-casting lights in a scene at fixed GPU cost using reservoir-based importance sampling (ReSTIR).
    • DLSS (Deep Learning Super Sampling) — AI-based upscaling that renders at a lower resolution and uses a neural network to reconstruct a higher resolution image, recovering much of the performance cost of ray tracing.
    • NRD (NVIDIA Real-Time Denoisers) — A denoising library specifically designed for real-time ray traced outputs, reducing the noise from low sample counts to a clean image.
    • RTX Mega Geometry — Introduced in NvRTX 5.6, this allows real-time path tracing of Nanite-level geometry by clustering millions of triangles into reusable structures, reducing CPU overhead and VRAM usage in heavy ray traced scenes.
    • Enhanced Ray Traced Translucency — Improved handling of transparent and translucent surfaces under ray tracing, addressing limitations in standard UE translucency rendering.

    NvRTX can increase hardware ray traced and path traced operations by up to 40% compared to the standard UE branch on compatible NVIDIA hardware.
