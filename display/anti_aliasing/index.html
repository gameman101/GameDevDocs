    Anti-Aliasing

    Introduction

    Anti-aliasing is the process of smoothing jagged edges that appear on rendered geometry. These jagged edges, often called "jaggies" or "staircasing", occur because a monitor is made up of a fixed grid of square pixels. When a diagonal or curved edge is drawn across that grid, the pixels can only approximate the shape, resulting in a stepped, rough appearance.

    The simplest way to understand anti-aliasing is to think of it as selectively blurring only the edges of objects rather than the entire image. The goal is to make edges appear smooth and continuous without noticeably softening the rest of the scene.

    Why It Matters

    Aliasing is most obvious on: - Thin diagonal lines and geometry edges - Fine details like fences, wires, and vegetation - Text and UI elements at low resolutions - Fast moving objects where edges shimmer and crawl

    Anti-aliasing reduces all of these artifacts, making images look cleaner and more stable especially in motion. Different techniques trade off between image quality, performance cost, and specific types of artifacts they introduce or solve.

    Traditional / Sampling-Based Methods

    SSAA — Supersampling Anti-Aliasing

    The oldest and most brute-force approach. The entire scene is rendered at a multiple of the target resolution (2x, 4x) and then downsampled to fit the display. Because the pixel shader runs on every sample, SSAA produces the highest possible image quality and handles all types of aliasing including texture aliasing.

    The final pixel color is the average of all the samples taken, resulting in extremely clean edges and sharp textures.

    Pros: Best possible image quality, handles all aliasing types naturally.

    Cons: Extremely expensive. 4x SSAA renders 4x as many pixels, nearly quartering your framerate. Rarely used in real-time rendering for this reason.

    MSAA — Multisample Anti-Aliasing

    The most well-known traditional anti-aliasing method. MSAA improves on SSAA by only supersampling at geometry edges rather than the entire image. It takes multiple coverage and depth samples per pixel around triangle edges, but only runs the pixel shader once per pixel.

    Available in multipliers: 2x, 4x, 8x. Higher multipliers take more samples per edge pixel and produce smoother results at greater performance cost.

    Pros: Much cheaper than SSAA while producing clean geometric edge anti-aliasing. No blurring of texture detail.

    Cons: Does not anti-alias shader-based aliasing (specular highlights, alpha tested geometry like leaves and fences). Incompatible with deferred rendering pipelines, which is the standard for most modern games. This incompatibility is the main reason MSAA has largely disappeared from modern titles.

    CSAA / EQAA — Coverage Sampling Anti-Aliasing

    Extensions of MSAA that add additional coverage samples on top of the standard color and depth samples. CSAA is NVIDIA's implementation and EQAA is AMD's equivalent.

    By increasing coverage samples without a proportional increase in color samples, these methods attempt to improve edge quality beyond standard MSAA with a smaller performance penalty. In practice the quality improvement over standard MSAA is modest and these techniques are rarely explicitly exposed to users anymore.

    Post-Process Methods

    These techniques are applied as a screen-space shader pass after the scene has been fully rendered. They do not have access to geometry or depth information beyond what is in the final image.

    FXAA — Fast Approximate Anti-Aliasing

    Developed by Timothy Lottes at NVIDIA. FXAA detects edges in the final rendered image using luminance contrast and applies a blur filter along those edges. It is implemented as a single pixel shader pass and runs entirely on the GPU.

    Because it works on the final image rather than geometry, FXAA catches aliasing that MSAA misses including shader aliasing, alpha tested edges, and specular highlights. It is also extremely cheap to run.

    Pros: Very fast, catches all types of edge aliasing, works with any rendering pipeline.

    Cons: Uses simple luma-based edge detection which is imprecise. Can over-blur non-edge areas and soften texture detail across the entire image. Considered outdated by modern standards as better alternatives exist at similar cost.

    MLAA — Morphological Anti-Aliasing

    AMD's post-process anti-aliasing technique. MLAA detects edge patterns in the image and reconstructs smooth edges by blending pixels based on the detected pattern shape. It produces results similar to or slightly better than FXAA but runs on the CPU rather than GPU in its original form, which limits its practical use.

    Pros: Better edge pattern recognition than FXAA.

    Cons: CPU-based in original form, slower than FXAA, largely superseded by SMAA.

    SMAA — Subpixel Morphological Anti-Aliasing

    A GPU-based improvement on MLAA that combines morphological edge detection with subpixel accuracy. SMAA uses more precise edge detection than FXAA, locating actual geometric edges rather than relying purely on luminance differences. It blends pixels based on detected edge patterns with higher accuracy.

    SMAA also handles subpixel aliasing — very fine details smaller than a pixel — which FXAA and basic MLAA miss. SMAA 4x adds spatial and temporal supersampling on top of the base technique for even higher quality.

    Pros: Better image quality than FXAA with less blurring, runs on GPU, works with deferred rendering.

    Cons: Slightly more expensive than FXAA. Does not handle temporal aliasing (shimmering in motion) as well as TAA. Has largely been replaced by TAA in most modern games.

    CMAA — Conservative Morphological Anti-Aliasing

    Intel's post-process anti-aliasing technique. CMAA is designed to be faster than SMAA while still providing better quality than FXAA. It focuses on the most visually impactful aliasing first using a conservative approach to edge detection, skipping complex cases that contribute little visible improvement.

    Rarely seen as an explicit option in games but used internally in some Intel-focused implementations.

    Temporal Methods

    Temporal anti-aliasing techniques accumulate information across multiple frames over time to build up a cleaner image than any single frame could provide alone.

    TAA — Temporal Anti-Aliasing

    The dominant anti-aliasing technique in modern games. Each frame the camera is jittered slightly (sub-pixel offset) so that different sample positions are covered over time. The results of the current frame and previous frames are blended together using a history buffer.

    This temporal accumulation effectively provides supersampling quality over time without the per-frame cost of SSAA. TAA handles all aliasing types including specular, shader, and geometric aliasing, and works perfectly with deferred rendering pipelines.

    Pros: Excellent quality, handles all aliasing types, compatible with all rendering pipelines, relatively cheap, backbone of modern upscaling technologies.

    Cons: Ghosting artifacts where moving objects leave a blurry trail from previous frames being carried over. Image can appear soft or blurry especially on fine detail. Requires a good velocity buffer and reprojection to minimize artifacts. Many games include a sharpening pass alongside TAA to counteract the softness.

    TXAA — Temporal Anti-Aliasing (NVIDIA)

    NVIDIA's branded implementation of temporal anti-aliasing combined with MSAA samples. TXAA uses MSAA as its base sample source and feeds those samples into a temporal accumulation filter. This produces higher quality results than standard TAA but at greater performance cost due to the MSAA component.

    TXAA was introduced with NVIDIA's Kepler architecture. It tends to be softer than standard TAA but with fewer ghosting artifacts. Less commonly seen in modern games as standard TAA implementations have improved significantly.

    AI / Machine Learning Methods

    DLAA — Deep Learning Anti-Aliasing (NVIDIA)

    NVIDIA's AI-based native resolution anti-aliasing. DLAA uses the same neural network as DLSS but runs at native resolution with no upscaling. It uses Tensor Cores on RTX GPUs to run a convolutional neural network that identifies and eliminates temporal artifacts and aliasing using learned heuristics rather than manually programmed rules.

    DLAA produces higher quality results than standard TAA implementations, preserving fine detail while removing ghosting and shimmering. Unlike DLSS it provides no performance benefit since it renders at full resolution — the performance cost is the neural network inference itself.

    Requires: NVIDIA RTX GPU (Tensor Cores required).

    Pros: Best anti-aliasing quality available at native resolution, eliminates most TAA artifacts, sharp and clean results.

    Cons: NVIDIA RTX exclusive, slight performance cost from neural network.

    DLSS — Deep Learning Super Sampling (NVIDIA)

    Primarily an upscaling technology but includes anti-aliasing as a core component of its reconstruction process. The game renders at a lower resolution (e.g. 1080p for a 1440p output) and DLSS uses a neural network to reconstruct the full resolution image, incorporating temporal information to produce a result that often matches or exceeds native resolution quality.

    Because DLSS includes temporal anti-aliasing in its reconstruction, enabling DLSS replaces any separate AA setting. Running DLSS and TAA simultaneously causes excessive blur from double temporal processing.

    Requires: NVIDIA RTX GPU.

    FSR — FidelityFX Super Resolution (AMD)

    AMD's upscaling solution with built-in anti-aliasing. FSR 1.0 was a spatial-only upscaler with no temporal component. FSR 2.0 and later use temporal accumulation similar to DLSS. FSR 3.0 introduced a native AA mode (FSRAA) that runs the FSR upscaler at native resolution purely for anti-aliasing, equivalent to DLAA but using AMD's algorithm.

    Unlike DLSS, FSR does not require specific GPU hardware and works on any modern GPU including NVIDIA and Intel cards.

    XeSS — Xe Super Sampling (Intel)

    Intel's upscaling and anti-aliasing solution. On Intel Arc GPUs XeSS uses XMX AI acceleration hardware for highest quality. On other GPUs it falls back to a DP4a compute shader path that still produces good results. XeSS 1.3 introduced XeSSAA (also called XeAA), a native resolution anti-aliasing mode equivalent to DLAA and FSRAA.

    Quick Comparison

    Technique Type Performance Cost Quality Works with Deferred
    SSAA Spatial Very High Highest Yes
    MSAA Spatial High High No
    CSAA / EQAA Spatial Medium-High High No
    FXAA Post-Process Very Low Low Yes
    MLAA Post-Process Low Low-Medium Yes
    SMAA Post-Process Low Medium Yes
    TAA Temporal Low High Yes
    TXAA Temporal + MSAA Medium-High Very High No
    DLAA AI Temporal Low-Medium Best Yes
    DLSS AI Upscaling + AA Performance gain Very High Yes
    FSR 2+ Temporal Upscaling + AA Performance gain High Yes
    XeSS AI/Temporal Upscaling + AA Performance gain High Yes

    Which Should You Use

    • Best quality, no upscaling → DLAA (NVIDIA RTX) or FSRAA / XeAA
    • Best quality with performance gain → DLSS / FSR 2+ / XeSS
    • No AI hardware, modern game → TAA
    • Older game with forward renderer → MSAA 4x
    • Lowest performance cost → FXAA
    • Middle ground without blur → SMAA

    If you are using DLSS, FSR, or XeSS for upscaling you do not need a separate anti-aliasing setting. The upscaler handles anti-aliasing as part of its reconstruction. Enabling both TAA and an upscaler simultaneously will cause excessive blurring.
