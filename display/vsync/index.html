    Vertical Sync (VSync)

    Vertical Sync, commonly known as VSync, is a display technology that synchronizes the frame rate output of your GPU with the refresh rate of your monitor. It was originally developed to eliminate screen tearing.

    What is Screen Tearing?

    Screen tearing happens when the GPU sends a new frame to the monitor before it has finished displaying the previous one. This causes a visual artifact where the screen appears horizontally split, showing parts of two different frames at the same time. It is most noticeable in fast moving scenes.

    How VSync Works

    Your monitor refreshes at a fixed rate, measured in Hz (e.g. 60Hz = 60 times per second). Your GPU renders frames as fast as it can, which may be faster or slower than that refresh rate. VSync forces the GPU to wait until the monitor finishes displaying the current frame before sending the next one, keeping them in sync.

    Buffering

    Double Buffering

    The default method. Uses two buffers: - Front buffer → what the monitor is currently displaying - Back buffer → where the GPU is rendering the next frame

    When the GPU finishes a frame it waits for the monitor to finish its refresh cycle, then swaps the buffers. This eliminates tearing but forces the GPU to sit idle while waiting, which can cut performance nearly in half if frames take just slightly longer than the refresh interval. For example on a 60Hz monitor, if a frame takes just over 16.67ms the framerate drops all the way to 30 FPS instead of nearly 60.

    Triple Buffering

    Adds a third buffer so the GPU never has to sit idle. While one back buffer is waiting to be swapped to the front, the GPU keeps rendering into the second back buffer. This results in higher average framerates and smoother performance compared to double buffering. The tradeoff is slightly higher input lag and more VRAM usage.

    Note: In DirectX, triple buffering is implemented as render-ahead rather than true triple buffering. True triple buffering (non-blocking) is mainly available in OpenGL.

    Types of VSync

    Standard VSync (On)

    The classic implementation. Caps your framerate to the monitor's refresh rate and uses double buffering. Completely eliminates tearing when your GPU is faster than your refresh rate. However it introduces input lag and if your framerate drops even slightly below the refresh rate, it halves to the next divisor (e.g. 60 → 30 → 15).

    Best for: Single player games where image quality matters more than responsiveness.

    Adaptive VSync (NVIDIA)

    A hybrid mode that dynamically toggles VSync on and off depending on your framerate: - Above refresh rate → VSync turns ON to prevent tearing - Below refresh rate → VSync turns OFF to prevent the framerate halving and stuttering

    This avoids the worst of both worlds — no tearing when you have headroom, no massive framerate drops when you don't. It cannot fully eliminate tearing or input lag so it is a compromise solution.

    Best for: Games where your framerate fluctuates around your refresh rate.

    Adaptive VSync — Half Refresh Rate

    Works exactly like Adaptive VSync but uses half your refresh rate as the switching threshold instead of the full refresh rate. On a 60Hz monitor this means VSync activates at 30 FPS instead of 60 FPS.

    Best for: Very demanding games where your framerate is consistently around half your refresh rate (e.g. 30 FPS on a 60Hz monitor).

    Fast Sync (NVIDIA)

    Available on GTX 900 series and newer. Combines triple buffering with a non-blocking approach — the GPU renders freely without waiting, and the most recently completed frame is always picked for display, discarding any older waiting frames. This nearly eliminates tearing while keeping input lag very close to having VSync off entirely.

    The tradeoff is that it works best when your framerate is significantly higher than your refresh rate (ideally 2-3x). If your framerate is only slightly above your refresh rate, Fast Sync can actually introduce more latency than standard VSync.

    Best for: Competitive games where your GPU is very powerful and framerates are well above the refresh rate.

    Enhanced Sync (AMD)

    AMD's equivalent to NVIDIA's Fast Sync. Combines the triple buffering behavior of Fast Sync with the adaptive behavior of Adaptive VSync. When framerates drop below the refresh rate it disables sync entirely to avoid stuttering, similar to Adaptive VSync. When above the refresh rate it uses the latest completed frame like Fast Sync.

    Best for: AMD GPU users who want low latency without tearing.

    G-Sync (NVIDIA) / FreeSync (AMD)

    These are hardware-level adaptive sync technologies built into the monitor itself. Instead of locking the GPU to the monitor's refresh rate, they make the monitor dynamically match the GPU's output framerate. This eliminates both tearing and the input lag of traditional VSync simultaneously with no compromises.

    • G-Sync → NVIDIA proprietary, requires a G-Sync certified monitor
    • FreeSync → AMD open standard based on VESA Adaptive Sync, also supported by NVIDIA GPUs on compatible monitors

    Best for: Anyone who wants the best possible experience and has a compatible monitor.

    Input Lag Comparison

    From highest to lowest input lag:

    Method Input Lag Tearing
    VSync ON (double buffer, windowed) Highest None
    VSync ON (double buffer, fullscreen) High None
    Triple Buffering + VSync Medium-High None
    Adaptive VSync Medium Occasional
    Enhanced Sync / Fast Sync Very Low Rare
    VSync OFF (windowed) Very Low None (composited)
    VSync OFF (fullscreen) Lowest Yes
    G-Sync / FreeSync Lowest None

    When to Use What

    • Competitive multiplayer → VSync OFF or Fast Sync / Enhanced Sync
    • Single player, stable framerate → Standard VSync or G-Sync/FreeSync
    • Single player, unstable framerate → Adaptive VSync
    • Very demanding games at ~30fps → Adaptive VSync Half Refresh Rate
    • Best overall experience → G-Sync or FreeSync monitor

    Common Misconceptions

    "VSync always causes input lag" Standard VSync does, but Fast Sync and Enhanced Sync have input lag comparable to having VSync completely disabled.

    "Triple buffering always helps" In DirectX games triple buffering is actually render-ahead buffering which can add input lag. True non-blocking triple buffering is mainly an OpenGL feature.

    "G-Sync/FreeSync is just fancy VSync" No — traditional VSync locks the GPU to the monitor. G-Sync/FreeSync locks the monitor to the GPU. This is a fundamental difference that eliminates the tradeoffs entirely.
